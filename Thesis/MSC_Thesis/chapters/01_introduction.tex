\section{From a Lagrangian to a prediction}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

A particle physicist who proposes a new theory writes down a Lagrangian. An
experimentalist who wants to test that theory needs a cross section. Between
the two sits a long chain of steps, and the first link in that chain is
always the same: the Lagrangian must be converted into Feynman rules, the
elementary vertices from which every amplitude is assembled.

That conversion is purely mechanical. \Cref{sec:feynrules-algorithm} states
the recipe in full: take one term of the Lagrangian, attach a creation
operator for each field, commute the operators through, replace each
derivative by a momentum, and strip the external wavefunctions. There is no
insight required at any point. What is required is bookkeeping, and a great
deal of it. Every field carries Lorentz, spinor, colour, weak-isospin and
flavour indices that must be contracted with the right partner; every
covariant derivative expands into one term per gauge group under which the
field transforms; every field strength of a non-abelian group splits into
three pieces, so a product of $n$ of them splits into $3^n$; every exchange
of two fermions costs a sign whose convention must be fixed once and then
never violated.

For a Lagrangian with a handful of terms this is a long afternoon. For the
Standard Model it is a well-known but genuinely error-prone exercise. For
the Standard Model Effective Field Theory (SMEFT) it is not possible at all.
The model used as the main benchmark of this thesis contains $32$ Lagrangian
blocks and $298$ independent Wilson coefficients, and generates $184$
distinct reference vertices with three to six external legs. Nobody derives
that by hand, checks it by hand, or --- more to the point --- changes an
operator and rederives it by hand.

Automation is therefore not a convenience here; it is the only way the
calculation happens at all. And once the derivation is automated, a second
requirement appears immediately behind the first: the result has to be
trusted. An automated tool that silently contracts the wrong pair of indices
produces a plausible-looking vertex that is simply wrong, and no amount of
downstream machinery will notice. This thesis therefore treats derivation
and validation as one problem rather than two.

\section{The state of the art}
\label{sec:intro-state-of-the-art}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

The reference implementation of this idea is
\textsc{FeynRules}~\cite{Christensen:2008py,Alloul:2013bka}, a
\textsc{Mathematica} package that has been the standard model-building entry
point for collider phenomenology for well over a decade. Its workflow is the
one this thesis deliberately imitates, so it is worth stating explicitly.

A \textsc{FeynRules} model file is declarative. The user fills in
\texttt{M\$ClassesDescription} with the particle content, giving each field
its spin, its self-conjugacy, its indices and its quantum numbers; fills in
\texttt{M\$Parameters} with the couplings and mixing matrices; and declares
the gauge groups in \texttt{M\$GaugeGroups}, each with its coupling, its
gauge boson, its structure constants and its representations. The Lagrangian
is then written as an ordinary \textsc{Mathematica} expression using a small
vocabulary of symbols --- \texttt{DC[...]} for a covariant derivative,
\texttt{FS[...]} for a field strength, \texttt{del[...]} for an ordinary
derivative, \texttt{Ga[...]} for a Dirac matrix. A single call to
\texttt{FeynmanRules[...]} returns the vertices, and a further call exports
the model in the UFO format~\cite{Degrande:2011ua}, which is what event
generators such as \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~\cite{Alwall:2014hca} actually
consume.

\textsc{FeynRules} is one member of a larger ecosystem.
\textsc{FeynArts}~\cite{Hahn:2000kx} and
\textsc{FormCalc}~\cite{Hahn:1998yk} generate and evaluate diagrams from a
model file; \textsc{CalcHEP}~\cite{Belyaev:2012qa} couples rule generation to
a matrix-element generator; \textsc{SARAH}~\cite{Staub:2013tta} specialises
in supersymmetric model building and mass-spectrum generation;
\textsc{LanHEP}~\cite{Semenov:2014rea} solves the same Lagrangian-to-vertices
problem in a standalone C implementation. The common structure is a
declarative model description on one side, a symbolic engine in the middle,
and an interchange format on the other side.

Two practical observations motivated this project. The first is that almost
every tool in this list is either built on \textsc{Mathematica}, and so
inherits a commercial licence and a closed core, or is a standalone program
with its own input language. Neither choice sits naturally next to the
Python-based analysis and machine-learning stack that the field now uses for
almost everything else. The second is cost. Loading the SMEFT model used in
this thesis into \textsc{FeynRules} and exporting its reference vertices
takes roughly half an hour of wall-clock time, as recorded in
\Cref{tab:validation-performance}. That is tolerable once, and painful in a
loop where an operator is edited and the vertices are regenerated.

The gap this thesis addresses is therefore not that the problem is unsolved.
It is that the solution has not, until recently, been available in the
ecosystem where the rest of the work is done.

\section{Why Python, and why now}
\label{sec:intro-why-now}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

Python was for a long time a poor host for this kind of calculation. Not
because of the language, but because the symbolic expressions involved ---
tensor networks with typed indices, expanded over thousands of terms ---
were beyond what the available Python computer-algebra systems could handle
at an acceptable speed.

That has changed. \textsc{Symbolica}~\cite{symbolica} is a computer-algebra
system with a core written in Rust and a Python interface. It treats large
symbolic expressions as ordinary in-process objects, rewrites them through
wildcard pattern matching, normalises them through a fast multivariate
polynomial engine, and --- decisively for this work --- can reduce a tensor
expression with contracted dummy indices to a canonical form.
\textsc{Spenso}~\cite{spenso} adds the missing type information: it makes an
index not merely a name but a slot carrying a representation, so that the
library itself knows that a colour fundamental index may contract with its
dual and not with a Lorentz index. A companion layer, idenso, supplies the
physics identities on top --- metric contraction, $\mathrm{SU}(N)$ colour
algebra, and the Dirac algebra.

Together these three libraries supply exactly the services that a
\textsc{FeynRules}-class tool needs and that Python previously lacked.
\Cref{ch:cas} describes them in detail. The present thesis is, in one
sentence, an attempt to find out how far one can get by building on them.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

This thesis presents \textsc{FeynPy}, a Python toolkit that derives
tree-level Feynman rules from declarative model definitions. Its
contributions are the following.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A declarative model language in plain Python.} Indices,
    fields, parameters, gauge representations and gauge groups are ordinary
    Python objects, and a Lagrangian is written as an ordinary Python
    expression in a notation deliberately close to the
    \textsc{FeynRules} one. Nothing is a string to be parsed, and every
    intermediate object can be inspected in a debugger or a notebook
    (\Cref{ch:feynpy}).

    \item \textbf{A generic gauge compiler.} Covariant derivatives, field
    strengths, gauge-fixing terms and Faddeev--Popov ghosts are expanded
    from the model's own gauge metadata rather than from hard-coded
    templates. Because the expansion is generic, operators such as $F^3$,
    $F^4$, nested covariant derivatives and products mixing several gauge
    groups work without any additional code
    (\Cref{sec:internals-compiler}).

    \item \textbf{A contraction engine with a controlled fermion-sign
    convention.} Vertices are produced by a pruned sum over permutations of
    the external legs, the direct realisation of the canonical-quantisation
    recipe. Products of closed Dirac bilinears --- where the naive Grassmann
    sign disagrees with the \textsc{FeynRules} convention --- are handled by
    carrying the bilinear pairing recorded during lowering all the way into
    the engine (\Cref{sec:internals-contraction-fermions}).

    \item \textbf{A canonicalisation layer that makes cross-tool comparison
    possible.} Dummy relabelling, tensor symmetries, a deterministic
    generator-ordering rewrite and a restricted use of the Jacobi identity
    reduce two independently derived expressions to a common normal form
    (\Cref{sec:internals-postprocessing,sec:validation-canonical}). This was
    one of the hardest parts of the work and is what turns ``the two results
    look similar'' into a decidable statement.

    \item \textbf{An operator calculus on compiled Lagrangians.} Beyond
    vertex extraction, a compiled Lagrangian can be rewritten in place:
    field transformations implement electroweak symmetry breaking and the
    background-field split, and graded operators implement infinitesimal
    gauge variations and BRST transformations, so that gauge invariance and
    nilpotency become executable checks
    (\Cref{sec:feynpy-transformations,sec:feynpy-brst}).

    \item \textbf{A model-agnostic comparison framework, and three validated
    benchmarks.} The packaged Standard Model reproduces its
    \textsc{FeynRules} export at $163/163$ vertices; the unbroken
    background-field Standard Model at $67/67$; and the Green-basis SMEFT at
    $184/184$ reference lines, of which $161$ match directly and the rest
    after documented charge-conjugation conventions. Deliberate mutation
    tests confirm that the comparison rejects vertices whose physical
    content has been altered (\Cref{ch:validation}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and limitations}
\label{sec:intro-scope}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

It is worth being explicit about what this work does not do, so that the
results of \Cref{ch:validation} are read correctly.

\textsc{FeynPy} derives \emph{tree-level} Feynman rules. There is no
loop-level or next-to-leading-order functionality, and no counterterm
machinery. It produces \textsc{Symbolica} expressions, not model files:
there is no UFO, \textsc{FeynArts} or \textsc{CalcHEP} export, so the output
cannot yet be handed directly to an event generator. Restriction files, a
\textsc{FeynRules} feature used to switch parts of a model off, are not
ported. The integration-by-parts layer handles scalar fields only. General
open multi-fermion tensor structures are supported less completely than
closed Dirac bilinears.

The validation claim is correspondingly bounded. It compares interaction
vertices, not parameter-card semantics or numerical model metadata, and the
reference exports used here contain vertices with three to six external
legs, so two-point functions fall outside the comparison. These limits are
stated precisely in \Cref{sec:validation-coverage} and revisited in
\Cref{ch:conclusion}.

Within those bounds, the claim is a strong one: for three complete and
independently written models, the vertices produced by \textsc{FeynPy} agree
with those produced by \textsc{FeynRules} as algebraic expressions.

\section{Outline}
\label{sec:intro-outline}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

\Cref{ch:theory} collects the theory the rest of the thesis assumes: the
role of symmetry, the Lie-algebraic machinery that gives fields their
indices, the structure of gauge Lagrangians including gauge fixing, ghosts
and spontaneous symmetry breaking, the logic of effective field theory and
the SMEFT bases used later, and finally the canonical-quantisation algorithm
that turns a Lagrangian term into a vertex.

\Cref{ch:feynpy} is a user manual. It builds a model from indices and fields
up to a complete gauge-fixed QCD Lagrangian, extracts its vertices, and then
covers the further operations available on a compiled Lagrangian: field
transformations into a physical basis, gauge variations, BRST
transformations, and the packaged Standard Model and SMEFT models.

\Cref{ch:cas} introduces \textsc{Symbolica}, \textsc{Spenso} and idenso, and
explains the single small object that bridges them to the toolkit.

\Cref{ch:internals} opens the machine. It follows one Lagrangian term
through analysis, index resolution, gauge compilation, the intermediate
representation where every branch of the code meets, the Wick-contraction
engine, and canonicalisation, and closes with the problems that shaped the
design.

\Cref{ch:validation} is the evidence. It defines what ``equal'' means for
two independently derived tensor expressions, reports the three benchmark
comparisons, examines the charge-conjugation and chirality conventions that
required care, and demonstrates through mutation testing that the comparison
is not vacuous.

\Cref{ch:conclusion} summarises what was achieved, what proved difficult,
and where the work goes next.
