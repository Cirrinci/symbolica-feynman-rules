\chapter{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{ch:conclusion}

% --------------------------------------------------------------
\section{What was achieved}
\label{sec:conclusion-achievements}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

This thesis set out to find whether a \textsc{FeynRules}-class derivation of
Feynman rules can be carried out inside the Python ecosystem, and to
establish, rather than assert, that the answer it produces is correct. Both
questions now have answers.

\textsc{FeynPy} takes a model declared as ordinary Python objects --- index
types, fields, gauge representations, gauge groups, parameters --- together
with a Lagrangian written in a notation close to the one used on paper, and
returns tree-level Feynman rules as \textsc{Symbolica} tensor expressions.
The declaration is short: gauge-fixed QCD is a page of code
(\Cref{sec:feynpy-qcd-example}), and the seven vertex signatures it produces,
including the four-gluon vertex and the ghost--gluon coupling, follow from
four written terms.

The machinery underneath that surface is generic rather than templated. The
covariant derivative expands from the model's own gauge metadata, so a field
charged under three groups produces three currents without any code that
knows about the Standard Model; the field strength expands the same way, so
$F^3$, $F^4$ and mixed-group operators work without additional support. Gauge
fixing and Faddeev--Popov ghosts are generated from the group declaration.
The contraction engine implements the canonical-quantisation recipe of
\Cref{sec:feynrules-algorithm} directly, as a pruned sum over permutations of
the external legs, with a fermion-sign convention that is stated once and
enforced by construction.

Beyond extraction, a compiled Lagrangian is a manipulable object.
Field transformations turn the gauge basis into the physical basis, which is
how electroweak symmetry breaking and the background-field split are
implemented --- as substitutions, with no dedicated code path for either.
Graded operators implement infinitesimal gauge variations and the BRST
differential, so gauge invariance and $s^2=0$ become computations rather than
claims.

The validation is the part of the work I would defend most firmly. Comparing
two independently derived tensor expressions is not a matter of subtracting
them; it requires deciding what ``equal'' means when dummy indices are named
differently, factors are ordered differently, and the same object has been
expanded through different identities. \Cref{sec:validation-canonical} makes
that decision explicit, and on the resulting definition the packaged Standard
Model matches its reference export at $163/163$ vertices, the unbroken
background-field Standard Model at $67/67$, and the Green-basis SMEFT at
$184/184$ reference lines --- $161$ directly and the remainder under
charge-conjugation conventions that are documented per line rather than
absorbed silently. The mutation tests of
\Cref{sec:validation-sensitivity} confirm that this agreement is informative:
deliberately altering a sign, a coefficient or an index structure makes the
comparison fail.

\section{What proved hard}
\label{sec:conclusion-hard}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

\Cref{sec:internals-difficulty} records the engineering difficulties from
inside the implementation. Read from the outside, four of them are worth
restating, because they are the ones a reader attempting something similar
would meet again.

The first is that indices, not algebra, are where this problem is hard. A
field carrying two indices of the same kind breaks any scheme that keys
labels by kind alone, and the failure is silent: the wrong pair contracts,
the expression stays well formed, and the vertex is wrong. The eventual
design refuses to guess. An ambiguous index assignment is an error raised at
declaration time, and gauge representations state their slot policy
explicitly. Almost every hour spent debugging on this project was spent on
this class of problem.

The second is fermion signs. On paper the rule is one line; in an
implementation built on a commutative multiplication it is a piece of
information that must be threaded from the point where a Dirac bilinear is
recognised all the way into the contraction engine, and lost anywhere along
the way it cannot be recovered. Matching the \textsc{FeynRules} convention
for products of closed bilinears, without breaking generic multi-fermion
terms, took longer than writing the engine that uses it.

The third is that two correct tools disagree. The charge-conjugation
conventions of \Cref{sec:validation-ec} and the chirality-projector
redundancy of \Cref{sec:validation-chirality} are not errors on either side;
they are different, equally defensible packagings of the same physics. The
temptation is to add a transformation until the numbers match. The discipline
this thesis tried to keep is to allow exactly one global convention, to
tabulate every remaining case individually, and to state which is which.

The fourth is that generality has a cost that shows up somewhere else.
Replacing the special-case $F^2$ handler with a generic field-strength
expansion unlocked $F^3$ and $F^4$ for free, and simultaneously multiplied
the number of raw local monomials to the point where the contraction engine's
compatibility check became the bottleneck. Species-aware pruning fixed it,
but the lesson generalises: each layer made generic pushes work onto the
next.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

The claims of this thesis are bounded, and the boundaries are worth restating
in one place.

\textsc{FeynPy} derives tree-level rules only. There is no loop-level
functionality, no counterterms, and no renormalisation.

It produces symbolic expressions, not model files. Without a UFO exporter
the output cannot be handed to an event generator, which means the toolkit is
not yet a drop-in replacement for \textsc{FeynRules} in a phenomenology
workflow, however well its vertices agree.

The integration-by-parts layer handles unlabelled scalar species only
(\Cref{sec:feynpy-ibp}). This is the single most consequential gap, because
it is what would be needed to reduce a Green basis to the Warsaw basis, and
that reduction is the natural next thing to ask of a tool that can already
build a Green basis.

Restriction files are not ported, so switching off part of a model requires
editing the model. General open multi-fermion tensor structures are supported
less completely than closed Dirac bilinears. And the validation itself
compares interaction vertices, not parameter-card semantics or numerical
metadata; the reference exports contain vertices with three to six legs, so
two-point functions lie outside the comparison
(\Cref{sec:validation-coverage}).

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:conclusion-future}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

Several directions follow directly from the limitations above, roughly in
order of how much they would add relative to the effort required.

\paragraph{UFO export.} The most immediately useful addition, and the least
conceptually demanding. The vertices already exist in a canonical form with
typed indices; what is missing is a serialiser and a parameter-card model.
This is what would turn \textsc{FeynPy} from a derivation tool into a usable
front end for \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~\cite{Alwall:2014hca} and the rest
of the UFO ecosystem~\cite{Degrande:2011ua}.

\paragraph{A full integration-by-parts and equation-of-motion layer.}
Extending \py{ibp_normal_form()} beyond scalars, and adding the
equation-of-motion substitutions of \Cref{sec:theory-green-basis}, would let
the toolkit reduce a redundant Green basis to the Warsaw basis
automatically. Since the operator calculus of \Cref{sec:feynpy-operators} is
already the right shape for this --- a rewriting acting on compiled terms ---
the work is in the reduction algorithm rather than in the infrastructure.

\paragraph{The full $R_\xi$ gauge in broken theories.} Gauge fixing is
currently generated for unbroken groups. Extending it to the broken
electroweak sector, where the gauge-fixing functional mixes gauge bosons with
Goldstone fields and each mass eigenstate carries its own $\xi$, would make
the $\xi$-independence of physical results available as a routine internal
check rather than a manual one.

\paragraph{Loop level.} Propagators, counterterms and one-loop matching are
the largest extension, and the one where the choice of foundation should pay
off most: \textsc{Symbolica} and \textsc{Spenso} were built for exactly the
kind of large tensor expressions that loop calculations produce. Even
without a full loop implementation, generating the propagators and
counterterm structures that an external loop tool consumes would be a
worthwhile intermediate step.

\paragraph{Broader validation.} Three models is enough to be convincing and
not enough to be complete. Supersymmetric models, models with extended gauge
sectors, and dimension-eight SMEFT operators would each stress a different
corner of the compiler --- and, given the mutation-testing framework of
\Cref{sec:validation-sensitivity}, adding a benchmark is now largely a matter of
writing the model.

\section{Closing remark}
\label{sec:conclusion-closing}
% --------------------------------------------------------------

The thesis began with the observation that deriving Feynman rules is
mechanical, unforgiving, and --- for a theory the size of SMEFT --- no longer
something a person can do. The response was not to write a smarter
derivation, since the algorithm has been known for decades and is stated in
five lines in \Cref{sec:feynrules-algorithm}. It was to build the layers
around that algorithm carefully enough that it can be run on a real model and
believed: a declaration language that refuses ambiguity, a compiler that
expands gauge structure generically, one intermediate representation where
every path meets, a canonical form that makes equality decidable, and a
comparison that has been shown to fail when it should.

That the result reproduces \textsc{FeynRules} exactly on three complete
models is the evidence. That it does so from about a page of Python per
model, inside the ecosystem where the rest of the analysis already lives, is
the point.
